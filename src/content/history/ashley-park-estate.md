Ashley Park no longer exists as a house, but its former grounds still shape a whole corner of Walton, in the street names of a residential neighbourhood built directly over what was once one of the town's grandest private estates.

<!-- IMAGE: ashley-park-road-sign.jpg, alt: "Ashley Park Road street sign, Walton-on-Thames" -->

## The mansion and its owners

A country house stood on the Ashley Park site, in various forms, from around 1605 until the early 1920s. Its owners across that long span included Charles Sackville, styled Earl of Middlesex and later 2nd Duke of Dorset, in the eighteenth century, and, around the turn of the twentieth century, the Sassoon family, part of a major international trading dynasty. David Sassoon bought the estate in 1867, and it remained in his family until the final owner, Sassoon Joseph Sassoon, sold it in 1923.

## Viscount Shannon and the Roubiliac monument

Ashley Park's best-documented resident is Richard Boyle, 2nd Viscount Shannon, whose 1755 monument by the sculptor Louis-François Roubiliac survives inside [St Mary's Church](/history/st-marys-church/) in Walton, one of the finest single objects the parish church holds. That monument is now, in a real sense, the most substantial surviving trace of Ashley Park's occupants: the house they lived in is gone, but the memorial commissioned by one of them remains exactly where it was placed nearly two hundred and seventy years ago.

## Demolition and subdivision

Following the 1923 sale, the house was demolished within a few years and the grounds built over, with the bulk of the estate developed as detached housing through the interwar period. Some of that original interwar building survives in an Arts and Crafts idiom; much has since been rebuilt in a variety of later styles.

## The streets that replaced it

The clearest trace of the old estate today is in its street names. Ashley Park Road, Ashley Drive and Ashley Rise all sit within what was once the estate's grounds, a quiet piece of naming that keeps Ashley Park's memory alive on every letterbox and satnav in the area, long after the house itself disappeared.

## The golf club that came and went

Before any of the housing arrived, part of the estate had a much shorter-lived second life as Ashley Park Golf Club, which used the grounds from 1890. The club folded after seventeen years, holding its final dinner in May 1907, at which the estate's owner, Joseph Sassoon, was thanked for having allowed free use of his park for golf throughout the club's existence. It is a small footnote, but a genuine one: for nearly two decades, one of Walton's grandest private estates doubled as a golf course open to a paying membership, a use that has left no trace at all beyond the historical record.

## Ashley Park's other claim to fame

Ashley Park has one further, unexpected credential: the estate is the strongest candidate for the location of [an early recorded game of baseball in 1749](/history/origins-of-baseball/), played by a member of the same Sackville family that owned the house in that period. It means the same patch of ground that later gave its name to three residential streets may also hold a genuine claim to a moment in the early history of American baseball, discovered in the newspaper archives centuries after the game itself was played.
