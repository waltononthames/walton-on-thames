The name of the modern Borough of Elmbridge is not a piece of twentieth-century council branding. It is a genuinely ancient administrative name, inherited almost unchanged from an Anglo-Saxon land division that predates the Domesday Book.

<!-- IMAGE: river-mole-amelebrige.jpg, alt: "The River Mole, from which the Elmbridge hundred took its Saxon name" -->

## What a hundred was

In Anglo-Saxon England, a hundred was a subdivision of a shire used for taxation, justice and the organisation of local militia. Historians remain uncertain exactly what unit of measurement the "hundred" in the name originally referred to, whether a hundred households, a hundred men able to bear arms, or a hundred hides of land, but the hundred system as a whole formed the basic unit of local government below the level of the shire across most of Anglo-Saxon England, including Surrey.

## From Amelebrige to Elmbridge

The hundred covering [Walton](/history/domesday-and-origins/) and the surrounding area was recorded under the Old English name Amelebrige, combining an early form of the name of the River Mole, "Amele", with the Old English word for a bridge, "brige". Over the following centuries the name gradually shifted in pronunciation and spelling until it settled into the modern form Elmbridge, the crossing point on the Mole that gave its name to the whole surrounding hundred.

## An unusually persistent boundary

What makes Elmbridge worth noting is not just its age but its persistence. The modern Borough of Elmbridge, created in the 1974 local government reorganisation that also absorbed Walton-on-Thames as an urban district in its own right, corresponds almost exactly to the boundaries of the old Saxon hundred, a genuinely unusual degree of continuity for an administrative boundary to maintain across roughly a thousand years of subsequent reorganisation, war and change.

## Researching the hundred

The Elmbridge Hundred is also the name of an independent research project, hosted at elmbridgehundred.org.uk, dedicated to documenting the human heritage of the historic hundred in detail, well beyond what a single page here can cover. For readers who want to go further into the area's Saxon and medieval administrative history, it is the natural next stop.
