Walton's name is older than the town most people recognise today, and its first documentary appearance predates the Norman Conquest's own paperwork by only twenty years. The Domesday Book of 1086 is not where Walton begins, but it is the earliest point where the town's existence is written down in black and white.

<!-- IMAGE: domesday-book-facsimile.jpg — alt: "Facsimile page from the Domesday Book" -->

## A Celtic name

Walton's name is generally understood to derive from Old English elements associated with the Wealas, the word Anglo-Saxons used for the Britons who remained in England after their arrival, combined with tun, a settlement or farmstead. Read that way, "Walton" essentially means the settlement of the Britons, or of foreigners, from an Anglo-Saxon point of view, implying a pre-Saxon Celtic community here that the incoming Saxons recognised as distinct enough to name. It is a small piece of etymology, but it pushes Walton's story back before any of its written records, into the transition between Roman Britain and Anglo-Saxon England.

## The Saxon settlement

By the time Walton enters the documentary record, it sat within the Anglo-Saxon administrative unit known as the Elmbridge hundred, recorded in Old English as Amelebrige, a name combining the River Mole's early form with the Old English word for a bridge. That hundred's boundaries correspond closely to the modern Borough of Elmbridge, meaning the administrative geography visible on a council map today has genuinely Saxon roots. Read more about the [Elmbridge hundred itself](/history/elmbridge-hundred/).

## Waletona in 1086

Walton appears in the Domesday Book of 1086 as Waletona, held at the time by Edward of Salisbury and Richard de Tonbridge. The entry records six hides of land, a church, two mills worth £1 5s, a fishery worth 5s, fourteen ploughs, forty acres of meadow, and grazing sufficient for fifty hogs; the whole holding rendered £28 to its lords. That church is almost certainly the direct ancestor of [St Mary's Church](/history/st-marys-church/), meaning Walton's parish church has a documented existence stretching back, in some form, to the eleventh century and probably earlier.

## What survives

Very little of Domesday Walton is visible above ground today. The mills and fishery are long gone, and the settlement pattern the entry describes has been overwritten many times since by medieval, Georgian, Victorian and modern building. What survives is mostly documentary and etymological: the name itself, the hundred it sat within, and the outline of a working Thames-side settlement, with a church, mills and a fishery, that was already established enough by 1086 to be worth recording in detail. It is not a dramatic inheritance, but it is a genuine one, and it means Walton's history properly starts centuries before Cowey Stakes, the film studios or any of the town's later, more visible chapters.
