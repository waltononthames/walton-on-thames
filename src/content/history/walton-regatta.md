Walton's regatta is one of the oldest things that still happens here. The event now called Walton & Weybridge Regatta describes itself as formerly Walton Amateur Regatta, and as held annually since 1862 on the Thames at Walton-on-Thames (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.b). Weybridge Rowing Club, which shares the running of it, uses the same wording (Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.b).

That date matters locally, because it makes the regatta older than either of the clubs now most closely associated with it. Weybridge Rowing Club dates from 1881, and Walton Rowing Club was formed in 1927 with Steve Fairbairn as its first president (Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.b; Walton Rowing Club, n.d.). The regatta was not created by a club and then opened to the public. It was already here, and the clubs grew up around it.

What follows is what the surviving evidence supports: a Victorian riverside spectacle that drew crowds onto private lawns, a town that ran two regattas at once because the rules of amateur sport excluded working men, an Olympic crew who came home from Paris in 1924 and won on this reach, and a modern competitive regatta that still races the same water.

## How firm is the 1862 date?

Two independent strands point to 1862. The present organisers state it, and the local historian John Stonebanks, writing for the Walton & Weybridge Local History Society, also dates the first Walton Regatta to that year (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.b; Stonebanks, 1980).

Stonebanks adds something the modern phrasing does not. He records that the regatta began in 1862 but became an annual fixture only after several lapses (Stonebanks, 1980). Both statements can stand together if "held annually since 1862" is read as a claim about an institution rather than a guarantee that racing took place in every calendar year. The two world wars alone make the stricter reading impossible: the Skiff Racing Association's history records that regattas and the skiff marathon races lapsed during the First World War, and that competitions lapsed again during the Second and were not revived until afterwards (Skiff Racing Association, n.d.).

The founding year is well attested by later writers. No contemporary programme, results list or newspaper report of the 1862 meeting has been traced for this article, so the detail of that first regatta remains unknown.

## Before 1862: river spectacle at Oatlands

The word regatta once described a river entertainment as readily as a rowing competition, and Walton's reach had seen both.

Stonebanks records an eighteenth-century regatta connected with Oatlands, and another on 7 August 1775, when the Duke of Newcastle staged a large river entertainment there attended by members of the royal family. He is careful to distinguish these social occasions from sporting regattas in the later competitive sense (Stonebanks, 1980).

That distinction is worth keeping. The 1775 event does not make Walton's regatta older than 1862. What it shows is that this stretch of the Thames was already understood as a place where a crowd could be gathered and something staged on the water.

## Why the river, and why then

The timing of 1862 fits the wider opening up of the Thames. The railway had reached Walton in 1838, putting the town within easy reach of London (Network Rail, 2024), and the middle of the century saw rowing clubs, boat builders, hotels and riverside pubs turn a working river into a place people travelled to for pleasure.

Walton had a practical asset as well: a long, straight reach with room to race, and banks from which a race could be watched from end to end. Almost everything else about the regatta has changed. The water has not.

## Two silver prizes and a swan

Two Victorian trophies survive in photographs, and both carry the same device: a swan inside a roundel lettered WALTON ON THAMES REGATTA, with a pair of oars crossed behind it.

The first is a silver goblet, hallmarked in London in 1864 and marked by the maker EM/JM, standing 19.5cm high, its bowl mounted with an applied cartouche carrying the device (Invaluable, n.d.). Because that cartouche is cast and applied rather than engraved afterwards, the hallmark dates the device as well as the silver. The second is an undated silver jug carrying the same device engraved, above the name of the event, LOCAL GIGS, and the winning crew: E. R. Kennedy and F. A. Stringer, with A. Payne as cox (Walton-on-Thames.org, n.d.).

The same device on two prizes of quite different manufacture is stronger evidence than either object alone. It points to a badge the regatta used rather than an ornament chosen once. We examine both objects, their marks and their limits in our history of [the swan as a symbol of Walton](/history/swan-symbol-walton-on-thames/).

## Amateurs, watermen and a second Walton regatta

In Victorian rowing, "amateur" was not simply the opposite of professional. It was a social boundary.

The National Amateur Rowing Association was established in 1890 for the large number of working-class men who were prevented from joining the Amateur Rowing Association by virtue of their manual occupations, and who were therefore treated as professionals under ARA rules whether or not they had ever been paid to row (The London Archives, n.d.). On the Thames this was especially pointed, because the watermen who worked the river were among the most skilful oarsmen on it.

Walton ran both sides of that divide. Stonebanks records that a Walton Town Regatta was inaugurated in about 1887, and that its events were open to watermen and others who did not hold amateur status (Stonebanks, 1980). This was not an alternative name for Walton Amateur Regatta. It was a separate meeting for the people the amateur code shut out.

The NARA was reconstituted in 1930, incorporated as a non-profit company in 1939, and dissolved in June 1956 when it merged with the ARA into a single governing body (The London Archives, n.d.). For most of the period covered by this article, then, British rowing did not speak with one voice, and Walton's reach gave both voices somewhere to race.

## River House, Sullivan and the Venetian fête

By the late nineteenth century the regatta was as much a social occasion as a sporting one, and it depended on the goodwill of the people who owned the riverbank.

Stonebanks records that from about 1887 successive owners of River House, downstream of [Walton Bridge](/history/walton-bridge/), made their riverside lawn available to the regatta's organising committees, a precedent set by John Brown and continued by later owners (Stonebanks, 1980).

The best-known of those later owners was Sir Arthur Sullivan, composer with W. S. Gilbert of the Savoy operas, who occupied River House during the 1890s. Stonebanks records that at the 1894 amateur regatta Sullivan's lawn was again placed at the organisers' disposal, that the band of the 2nd Battalion Rifle Brigade played music including Sullivan's own, and that a local newspaper afterwards recalled the day as one of the great celebrations of the boating season, anticipated in Walton much as Henley week was elsewhere (Stonebanks, 1980).

That comparison records a feeling rather than a ranking. It is evidence of how the day was experienced in Walton, not evidence that Walton rivalled Henley Royal Regatta in sporting standing, and it should not be read as the latter.

The most theatrical episode came in July 1897, when a Venetian fête was held on the river below Walton Bridge, with illuminated boats and a firework display, and with Sullivan as president of the organising committee (Stonebanks, 1980). Illuminated fêtes of this kind were a recognisable feature of late-Victorian Thames leisure. At Walton the river was a racecourse by day and a lit stage by night.

## Where people watched

The shape of the course itself could move to suit the spectators.

Stonebanks notes occasions when the lawn of [Mount Felix](/history/mount-felix/), and later that of Hillrise, was made available for viewing, and that when those lawns were used the finishing line could be shifted upstream from Walton Wharf so that the crowd had a better view (Stonebanks, 1980).

Wendy Hughes's photographic history of the town reproduces a view from around 1908 and records that the grounds of Mount Felix were opened for the day, with chairs provided and sometimes a band. The town joined in: window boxes were decorated, and banners and streamers were hung across High Street and Bridge Street (Hughes, 2003).

Regatta day, on that evidence, was not a private meeting held at the water's edge. It was something the town dressed for.

## War, and the return of racing

Organised rowing lapsed during the First World War (Skiff Racing Association, n.d.), but Walton was racing again within two years of the armistice, and the proof is photographic rather than recollected.

The Thames Rowing Club archive holds prints labelled "Walton 1920 - winners of Walton Fours 1920" and "Walton 1920 - winning crew senior eights", followed by "Walton 1921 - final Walton fours, won by TRC" and "Walton 1921 - senior eights final, Thames winning from London" (Thames Rowing Club Archive, n.d.b). A further print of 1934, taken by the Sport and General Press Agency, is catalogued as "Walton - Junior Senior fours" and described as heat A, Thames RC winning from London RC (Thames Rowing Club Archive, n.d.b).

These are contemporary records made by a participating club, and they establish something later reminiscence cannot: that leading metropolitan crews were racing at Walton in the immediate post-war years, and again in the 1930s.

## 1924: the Olympic crew who came home and won

The regatta's clearest connection to international sport belongs to the summer of 1924.

Five Weybridge Rowing Club men rowed for Great Britain in the coxed fours at the Paris Olympic Games: Vince Boveington, Bernard Croucher, Thomas Monk and John "Jack" Townend, with Harry Barnsley as coxswain. Weybridge had also contested the trial to select Britain's eight, and had been beaten by Thames Rowing Club (Olympedia, n.d.b). In Paris the four finished second in their heat and second again in the repêchage, the second-chance race for crews that have not qualified from their heat, and did not reach the final (Olympedia, n.d.a; Olympedia, n.d.b).

Their summer did not end there. Olympedia's biographies of Croucher and Townend both record that the same crew won the NARA Senior Fours, for the Townend Cup, at the Walton-on-Thames Regatta a couple of weeks later (Olympedia, n.d.b; Olympedia, n.d.c). Barnsley's biography records a second Walton success two months after the Games, when he won the NARA coxed double sculls there with W. A. Wigmore and Jack Townend (Olympedia, n.d.a).

Weybridge Rowing Club's own account of the crew sets that in its institutional context. The club belonged to the NARA, and states that this was the first time a NARA crew had been selected for the Olympics, and that the crew were afterwards excluded from Leander Club and from major regattas closed to them until the NARA and ARA merged (Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.a). That account is the club's own, written about its own members, and is presented here as such.

Two details remain unresolved. The sources do not agree on every name: Weybridge's page gives the crew as Joe Barnsley, Vince Bovington, Harry Monk, Jack Townend and Bernard Croucher, where Olympedia gives Harry Barnsley, Vince Boveington, Thomas Monk, John Townend and Bernard Croucher (Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.a; Olympedia, n.d.a, n.d.b, n.d.c). This article follows Olympedia. And none of the sources explains who or what the Townend Cup was named for, or whether the meeting the crew won at was formally Walton Amateur Regatta, Walton Town Regatta, or a NARA meeting under another title. The location and the NARA connection are secure. The title of the meeting is not.

John Townend did not live long after his Olympic summer. He was born on 6 September 1903 and died on 2 August 1926, aged twenty-two (Olympedia, n.d.c).

## A regatta day in 1949

The Thames Rowing Club Journal for 1949 preserves the closest thing we have to a full picture of a mid-century Walton regatta.

It records Walton Amateur Regatta on 11 June 1949, and lists the events Thames entered: the Walton Challenge Cup for Thames Cup eights, the Sabin Challenge Cup for junior-senior eights, the Miskin Challenge Cup for junior eights, the Goring Challenge Cup for Wyfold fours, a Thames Rowing Club Challenge Cup for pair oars, and junior-senior sculling (Thames Rowing Club, 1949, p. 9).

The racing itself comes through in the margins. Thames beat Westminster Bank Rowing Club by one and a quarter lengths in heat C of the Walton Challenge Cup, in 4 minutes 55 seconds, then lost heat F to Quintin Boat Club by three feet, in 4 minutes 54 seconds. Their Goring Challenge Cup four went out to Westminster Bank on a foul (Thames Rowing Club, 1949, p. 9).

The same journal explains where one of those trophies came from. Thames had decided to lend pieces of its own club plate to regattas for use as challenge cups, and reported that "a second and very handsome silver-gilt cup has been loaned to Walton Regatta to be used as a cup for the Senior Pairs" (Thames Rowing Club, 1949, p. 23). A cup competed for at Walton, in other words, had come out of a London club's silver safe.

Thames Rowing Club's archive also holds a dedicated Walton Amateur Regatta file, GB TRCA TRC-RAC-1-3, spanning 1949 to 1975 and comprising printed booklets for 1949, 1951, 1954 and 1975 (Thames Rowing Club Archive, n.d.a). It is a small collection, but it is the best run of Walton programmes we have traced.

One name in the 1949 list recurs elsewhere in Walton's rowing history. In 1951 Walton Rowing Club acquired the lease of its plot at Sunbury Lane from the Miskin family trust, and built the old boathouse there in 1953 (Walton Rowing Club, n.d.). Whether the Miskin Challenge Cup and the Miskin family trust are connected is not established by any source consulted for this article, and the recurrence of the name is offered here as a question rather than an answer.

## Skiffs, and a different Walton regatta

Skiff racing came into the regatta late. The Skiff Racing Association records that skiff events were included in Walton Amateur Regatta from 1994, and that the regatta became affiliated to the association in 1998 (Skiff Racing Association, n.d.).

That is worth separating from another event with a confusingly similar name. Walton Reach Regatta is a different meeting, staged by Thames Valley Skiff Club, and was affiliated to the Skiff Racing Association in 1983 (Skiff Racing Association, n.d.). Its 2026 running was held on Saturday 22 August over the Sunbury reach, from Walton Rowing Club to the Black Swan Sea Cadets (Environment Agency, 2026). Two regattas on overlapping water is not a contradiction. It is a measure of how heavily this stretch of the [Thames at Walton](/history/river-thames-at-walton/) has been used for sport.

## From Walton Amateur to Walton & Weybridge

The modern name was in use by the summer of 2007. The organisers' own report of that year is headed "St George's College defeat local rivals" and opens by recording that "Last Saturday Walton & Weybridge Regatta enjoyed a near perfect day with a record entry of 250 crews and windfree, sunny conditions", with 33 events raced at three-minute intervals from 8.30am to 7.30pm on 9 June (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, 2007).

Both the regatta and Weybridge Rowing Club describe the present event as formerly Walton Amateur Regatta, which is a direct statement of continuity between the two names (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.b; Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.b). What the available evidence does not establish is when exactly the change was made, or why. It also gives no support to the idea that two separate historic regattas were merged to create it. Committee minutes, notices of regatta or programmes from the years before 2007 would settle the question.

The name has its own logic on the ground. The river has never respected the boundary between the two towns, and the 1924 story makes the point plainly: Weybridge oarsmen won at Walton because the sporting river joined the two communities more readily than the map divided them.

## The regatta today

The course now runs about 1,000 metres, starting just below Walton Marina and finishing downstream around Walton Rowing Club (Weybridge Rowing Club, n.d.b). Much of it can be followed from the towpath.

The organisers' online results archive begins in 2007 (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.b). In 2026 the regatta was run over two days, on Saturday 6 and Sunday 7 June, with results published for both (Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.a; Walton and Weybridge Regatta, n.d.b).

Almost everything around the racing has changed. The private lawns are gone, the class rules that produced two regattas were dissolved in 1956, the boats and the categories would be unrecognisable to a Victorian crew, and the name itself is different. The racing is what carried through.

## Where the research could go next

Four questions would repay archival work, and we would welcome help with any of them.

**The first regatta.** No contemporary account of the 1862 meeting has been traced. Surrey and London newspapers from the weeks around it are the obvious place to look for a programme, a results list or a committee notice.

**Walton Town Regatta.** Stonebanks gives a date of about 1887 and describes who could enter. Contemporary programmes and press reports would show who organised it, what was raced for, and how long it ran alongside the amateur regatta.

**The 1924 meeting.** The Olympic connection is secure, but the meeting's formal title is not. NARA records at The London Archives, Weybridge Rowing Club's own minutes and the sporting press of September 1924 are the strongest prospects.

**The change of name.** Walton & Weybridge Regatta was in use by June 2007. Notices of regatta, programmes or committee minutes from the preceding years would fix the date and the reason.

These are gaps in the record rather than faults in it. Setting them out is how an evidence-led local history differs from a story tidied up to look complete.
