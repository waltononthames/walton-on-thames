## Our editorial promise

Walton-on-Thames.org will publish the strongest account that the available evidence can responsibly support. It will not make a claim more certain, more exact or more dramatic than the evidence permits.

Every historical article must allow a careful reader, editor or future researcher to answer four questions:

1. What is being claimed?
2. What evidence supports, qualifies or contradicts it?
3. How certain is the present conclusion?
4. Who reviewed and approved its publication?

These Standards translate the principles of *Our Charter* into repeatable research, writing, review and publication rules. They are intended for human editors, contributors, researchers, developers and AI-assisted workflows.

## 1. Authority, scope and interpretation

### 1.1 Governing hierarchy

For history and heritage, the governing hierarchy is:

1. *Our Charter*;
2. the Walton Historical Knowledge System (WHKS);
3. these Research and Editorial Standards and the Walton Knowledge Architecture (WKA);
4. Historical Projects;
5. the Publishing Pipeline; and
6. Walton-on-Thames.org.

Nothing in these Standards may override the Charter. If a conflict is found, the Charter prevails and these Standards must be amended.

The WHKS is the canonical evidence and research layer. It records sources, entities, atomic claims, evidence, events, relationships, uncertainty, review decisions and knowledge gaps. The WKA is the publishing layer. It turns approved knowledge into pages, timelines, maps, metadata, structured data and internal links.

The narrative article is never the final authority. The source and claim trail is the authority.

### 1.2 Content covered

These Standards apply to:

- historical and heritage articles;
- biographies and family histories;
- histories of buildings, estates, streets, businesses and organisations;
- historical timelines, maps, FAQs and captions;
- historical passages within otherwise current pages;
- source transcriptions, reconstructions and interpretive features;
- WHKS research and evidence records intended to support publication; and
- corrections to historical content.

Current opening hours, prices, transport details, business status, events and service listings are governed by separate Current Information Standards. A hybrid page must apply these Standards to its historical content and the Current Information Standards to its changing information.

### 1.3 Normative language

The following words have deliberate meanings:

- **Must** or **must not** identifies a mandatory requirement.
- **Should** or **should not** identifies the normal rule. A departure requires a recorded editorial reason.
- **May** identifies a permitted choice.

### 1.4 Key definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Substantive claim | A factual, inferential, interpretive or recollective proposition that contributes meaningfully to the account |
| Significant claim | A claim central to the title, conclusion, identity, chronology, location, ownership, origin, causation or reputation of a subject, or one likely to be repeated by others |
| Source observation | A precise record of what a source states, depicts or otherwise contributes, without overstating what it proves |
| Evidence | A source observation assessed in relation to a particular claim |
| Corroboration | Material support from genuinely independent evidence, not simple repetition of the same underlying account |
| Publication wording | The form in which an approved claim may responsibly appear in public |
| Human approval | A recorded decision by an accountable human editor, never by an automated tool |

## 2. Core editorial principles

Every piece of work must observe the following principles.

### 2.1 Evidence before assertion

A conclusion must be built from evidence. Research must not begin with a preferred story and then collect only material that supports it.

### 2.2 Accuracy before speed or reach

A publication deadline, search opportunity, topical anniversary or attractive narrative is never sufficient reason to lower the evidential threshold.

### 2.3 Precision must be earned

The level of precision in a claim must not exceed the level of precision in the evidence. An approximate site must not become an exact address. A year must not become a day. An early known appearance must not become an opening date.

### 2.4 Uncertainty is information

Uncertainty must be preserved, explained and made visible. It is not a defect to be edited away.

### 2.5 Conflicting evidence must remain visible

Credible evidence that weakens or contradicts a preferred conclusion must be recorded and assessed. It must not be silently omitted.

### 2.6 Interpretation must be identified

Historical writing inevitably involves selection and interpretation. Interpretive conclusions must be proportionate to the evidence and described as interpretation rather than disguised as fact.

### 2.7 Public usefulness follows historical integrity

Articles should be clear, engaging, accessible and easy to find. None of those goals permits exaggeration, invented detail or the removal of necessary qualifications.

### 2.8 Accountability remains human

Research, transcription, drafting and quality checks may be assisted by technology. The final decisions about evidence state, fairness, correction and publication must be made by an identifiable human editor.

## 3. Commissioning and research planning

### 3.1 A historical project must begin with a research brief

The brief should record:

- the central question or questions;
- the subject and relevant WHKS entities;
- geographical and chronological boundaries;
- the intended audience and public value;
- known prior work and existing Walton-on-Thames.org content;
- likely source classes and repositories;
- significant claims that will require enhanced scrutiny;
- sensitive, legal, privacy or copyright risks;
- expected deliverables;
- research gaps already known; and
- a practical stopping condition for the present phase.

The brief must not predetermine the conclusion.

### 3.2 Search activity must be recorded where completeness matters

For claims such as "first", "last", "only", "never", "oldest", "earliest" or "no record exists", the project must record:

- which collections, date ranges, catalogues and search terms were checked;
- which sources were inaccessible or incomplete;
- spelling variants and aliases used;
- whether OCR, indexing or catalogue limitations could hide relevant results; and
- the date on which the search was completed.

Normally, the responsible conclusion is "the earliest evidence found so far" rather than "the first", unless the search scope is sufficiently comprehensive to justify the stronger wording.

### 3.3 Research must preserve negative results and open questions

A failed search can be useful, but it does not normally prove that an event did not occur. Material negative results and unresolved checks must be entered as WHKS knowledge gaps rather than discarded.

## 4. Source registration and provenance

### 4.1 Every material source must be identifiable

Before it supports an approved claim, a source record must capture as much of the following as the item permits:

- creator, author, issuing body or advertiser;
- title or supplied description;
- publisher or repository;
- publication, creation or represented date;
- edition, issue, volume or sheet;
- original filename or physical identifier;
- archive, catalogue or collection reference;
- format and extent;
- whether the item is complete, cropped, excerpted or missing pages;
- the source's primary, secondary, mixed, reference or unknown role;
- rights and permission status;
- stable URL and access date where applicable;
- a file fingerprint where locally held; and
- any known transcription, reproduction or catalogue limitations.

Registration proves only that the source has been identified. It does not mean the source has been fully read, verified or accepted as reliable for every claim.

### 4.2 Source roles are question-specific

"Primary" and "secondary" describe a source's relationship to a question. They are not universal scores of quality.

For example:

- an advertisement is primary evidence that an advertiser publicly made a claim on a given date;
- the same advertisement may be weak evidence that every promotional statement was true;
- a modern redrawn estate plan is primary evidence of the modern author's reconstruction;
- the same plan is indirect evidence of the earlier landscape it attempts to reconstruct; and
- a later local history may be a strong synthesis, but its transcription of an older deed should not automatically be treated as equivalent to checking the deed itself.

### 4.3 Every source must be assessed against the claim it supports

The editor must consider:

| Test | Editorial question |
|---|---|
| Identity | Is this the correct item, edition, page, image, person or organisation? |
| Provenance | Where did it come from, and is the chain of custody or reproduction clear? |
| Proximity | How close was its creator to the event in time, place and participation? |
| Authority | Was the creator in a position to know this particular information? |
| Purpose | Why was the source created, and what might it omit, promote or distort? |
| Independence | Does it offer independent evidence, or repeat another account? |
| Completeness | Is the item whole, or could missing context change its meaning? |
| Precision | Does it support the exact date, name, address, relationship or extent claimed? |
| Legibility | Has the relevant text or visual detail been checked at adequate quality? |
| Consistency | How does it compare with other relevant evidence? |
| Reproducibility | Could another researcher find and inspect the same passage or feature? |
| Rights | May the source be quoted, reproduced or adapted for the proposed use? |

No single numerical score should replace this assessment. False precision in source scoring can conceal the reasons why evidence is strong or weak.

### 4.4 A working source priority

Researchers should normally seek the most authoritative and proximate evidence reasonably available, including:

1. original official, legal, archival or administrative records relevant to the question;
2. contemporary records produced by participants, owners, organisations or witnesses;
3. contemporary newspapers, directories, advertisements, maps, photographs and ephemera;
4. authoritative scholarly, museum, archive, heritage or specialist secondary work;
5. carefully sourced local histories and specialist community research;
6. recorded oral testimony, family material and community contributions;
7. tertiary summaries and low-authority web material used as discovery leads.

This is a research priority, not an automatic ranking of truth. A well-researched secondary work may correct a biased, incomplete or misunderstood primary source.

### 4.5 Sources that are not evidence by themselves

The following must not be used as the final authority for a historical claim:

- AI-generated answers or summaries;
- search-result snippets;
- OCR text that has not been checked against the source image;
- unattributed social-media posts;
- unsourced aggregations;
- copied text whose original source cannot be traced;
- crowdsourced assertions without supporting citations; or
- repetition across websites that ultimately derives from one unverified account.

Wikipedia may be used as a discovery map. It must not replace the reputable source required to support the published claim.

## 5. Rules for common historical sources

### 5.1 Official, legal and archival records

Official status does not make a record infallible. The editor must still establish what the record was designed to capture, who supplied the information and whether later amendments exist.

- A deed may establish a legal transaction but not necessarily the everyday name by which a site was known.
- A planning application proves that a proposal was submitted. It does not prove that permission was granted or the work completed.
- A listed-building entry is authoritative for the designation record at the stated date. Its descriptive history may still require checking against cited sources.
- A register may contain copying, spelling, age or reporting errors.
- A catalogue description is a finding aid unless the underlying item has been inspected.

Archive references and folio, item, page or image numbers must be recorded precisely.

### 5.2 Newspapers and periodicals

A newspaper may contain reporting, advertising, opinion, syndicated copy, official notices and later historical features on the same page. Each item must be classified by its own character.

The editor must distinguish:

- what the newspaper itself reported;
- what a named speaker or correspondent claimed;
- what an advertisement claimed;
- what an image visibly shows;
- what a caption states; and
- what the editor concludes after comparison with other evidence.

A contemporary report is evidence that the event was reported at that time. Where the claim is significant, surprising or reputationally sensitive, the underlying event should be corroborated if reasonably possible.

Syndicated or copied reports are not independent corroboration merely because they appeared in several titles.

### 5.3 Advertisements, directories and commercial notices

An advertisement can provide strong evidence that a named advertiser publicly used a business name, address, telephone number, product description, vacancy or offer on the publication date. It does not by itself prove:

- when the business began or ended;
- uninterrupted occupation before or after that date;
- legal ownership or tenancy of the premises;
- the truth of every promotional statement;
- the sale price of an advertised property;
- that an advertised development was completed; or
- that a vacancy proves expansion, contraction, opening or closure.

Directories are dated snapshots. Publication deadlines may precede the printed year, and entries can persist after circumstances change. The relevant directory methodology and coverage should be checked where possible.

### 5.4 Maps, plans and spatial evidence

Every map-based conclusion must identify:

- the map's date and, if different, the date represented;
- whether it is contemporary, copied, revised or reconstructed;
- title, sheet, edition, scale and orientation where known;
- the feature actually shown;
- the comparator used;
- the degree of alignment;
- relevant changes to roads, plots, numbering or boundaries;
- possible distortion, projection or georeferencing error; and
- the level of spatial precision the evidence can support.

The wording must match the spatial evidence:

- use "on the site of" only when site continuity is established;
- use "approximately on the site", "near", "within the former estate" or "in the same general area" when that is all the evidence permits;
- use "broadly follows the line of" for a non-exact route alignment; and
- do not publish exact coordinates derived from an approximate historic plan as though they were surveyed facts.

A visual resemblance between routes or boundaries is an inference until the comparison method and uncertainty have been recorded.

### 5.5 Photographs, film and visual evidence

A photograph is evidence of what the frame appears to show at the moment it was made. It may not establish the identity, date, ownership, function or wider context of every feature within it.

The editor must consider:

- photographer or creator;
- original caption and later captions;
- stated, estimated and inferred date;
- viewpoint and direction;
- crop, sequence and omitted surroundings;
- whether the image has been reversed, retouched, colourised or reconstructed;
- signs, clothing, vehicles, vegetation and architectural clues;
- whether the supplied image is an original, copy, scan or reproduction; and
- rights, permission and credit requirements.

A later caption is evidence of the captioner's identification, not automatically proof that the identification is correct.

### 5.6 Census, civil registration, parish and genealogical records

Names, ages, occupations, relationships and birthplaces may vary between records. The editor must:

- distinguish a recorded age from a calculated birth year;
- preserve meaningful spelling variants;
- avoid merging people because names are similar;
- test identity through several attributes where possible;
- distinguish residence from ownership;
- distinguish registration date from event date; and
- protect unnecessary personal information about living people.

A family tree without source citations is a research lead, not proof.

### 5.7 Built environment and planning records

Articles must distinguish among:

- the physical site;
- each building or structure on that site;
- the owner, developer, architect, occupier and operator;
- proposed, permitted, begun, completed and opened stages;
- demolition, conversion, extension and replacement; and
- formal designation from wider historical significance.

The survival of one lodge, wall, tree line or boundary feature must not be used to imply that an entire estate or layout survives unchanged.

### 5.8 Businesses and organisations

Articles must not merge the following merely for narrative convenience:

- a legal company;
- a trading name or brand;
- an owner or parent company;
- a partnership or sole trader;
- a shop or operating site;
- the building occupied;
- a successor business; and
- another business serving a similar market.

Site continuity, business continuity, brand continuity and legal succession are separate claims. Exact legal succession requires appropriate corporate, probate, partnership, property or transactional evidence.

### 5.9 Oral history, recollection and community knowledge

Oral testimony must record:

- the contributor's name or agreed form of identification;
- the interview or submission date;
- the contributor's relationship to the subject;
- whether the account is first-hand, inherited or repeated;
- the recording, transcript or written submission held;
- any timecode or section locator;
- consent, attribution and restriction terms; and
- known factors affecting memory or scope.

Personal recollection has historical value even when it cannot be corroborated. It must remain labelled as recollection unless other evidence supports a broader factual conclusion.

Anonymous testimony may guide research. It should not normally carry a controversial public claim without a compelling reason, adequate corroboration and explicit editorial approval.

### 5.10 Websites, databases and social media

For web sources, record the page title, responsible body, publication or update date where shown, URL and access date. Use an archived version where later change or deletion would materially affect reproducibility.

An official website is authoritative only within its competence. A museum page may be a strong summary but should not be assumed to reproduce every underlying source. A business website is primary evidence of the business's own current representation, not independent verification of its historical marketing claims.

Social media can establish that an account made a statement at a particular time. It does not establish that the statement is true. Identity and account authenticity must be checked.

## 6. Claims, evidence and certainty within the WHKS

### 6.1 One proposition per claim

Every WHKS claim should express one proposition that can be supported, qualified, contradicted or superseded independently.

Good:

> In 1867-68, the former private carriage road across Walton Lodge Park was laid out as a regular thoroughfare and became known as Sidney Road.

Too broad:

> Thomas Sidney bought the estates, created Sidney Road, developed the neighbourhood and named Bowes Road.

The broader sentence contains several propositions with different evidence and certainty.

### 6.2 Claim type, assertion status, confidence and review state are separate

The WHKS records four different editorial judgements:

| Field | Question answered |
|---|---|
| Claim type | Is this a fact, inference, interpretation or recollection? |
| Assertion status | Is it supported, provisional, disputed, rejected or unknown? |
| Confidence | How strong is the present editorial conclusion: high, medium or low? |
| Review state | Is the record draft, checked, approved or superseded? |

None of these fields substitutes for another. High confidence does not replace evidence. "Checked" does not mean "true". "Approved" means suitable for the permitted public use, not eternally immune from revision.

### 6.3 Evidence records must be claim-specific

Each materially distinct source observation must have:

- a source identifier;
- the claim it supports, rebuts or contextualises;
- an exact locator;
- direct, indirect or contextual strength;
- a concise observation;
- visual-check status where relevant;
- the check date; and
- any limitation affecting interpretation.

Conflicting evidence must be added as a separate record. It must not overwrite or erase the earlier observation.

### 6.4 Public evidence states

The Charter's public evidence states are editorial summaries derived from the full WHKS record. They are not produced from one field alone.

| Public state | Normal WHKS basis | Publication treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Verified | Approved and supported, with direct evidence or strong convergent evidence and no material unresolved conflict | May be stated as fact at the precision supported |
| Probable | Approved and supported, normally with a material limitation or medium confidence | Publish with explicit qualifying language |
| Uncertain | Commonly provisional or unknown, with incomplete evidence | Publish only when the uncertainty itself is useful, with a visible caveat and human approval |
| Disputed | Credible evidence or responsible interpretations conflict | Present the competing evidence and reasons for the provisional editorial view |
| Local tradition | A community memory or inherited account is evidenced, while the underlying event remains unverified | State that the tradition exists without converting the story into fact |
| Folklore or myth | A culturally significant story is documented but not established as history | Label clearly and explain its source or development where known |

A tradition can itself be verified as a tradition. That does not verify the event described by the tradition. The WHKS should hold those as separate claims.

### 6.5 Publication thresholds

One reliable and direct source may be sufficient for an ordinary, low-risk claim when the source is well suited to the question and no material conflict is known. A mechanical "two-source rule" must not replace judgement.

Enhanced scrutiny is required for:

- the origin of a place or street name;
- an exact location or claimed site continuity;
- ownership, authorship, responsibility or legal succession;
- "first", "last", "only", "oldest", "never" or other exhaustive claims;
- causal explanations;
- disputed identities;
- major dates that structure the narrative;
- surprising or exceptional claims;
- allegations, wrongdoing or reputational claims;
- claims about living people; and
- any claim used in the title, summary, FAQ, key-facts box or structured data.

For these claims, independent corroboration should be sought wherever reasonably possible. If it cannot be found, the limitation must be explicit and the wording reduced to the level the evidence supports.

### 6.6 Automatic and exceptional publication

The WKA may automatically use a claim only when:

1. `review_state` is `approved`;
2. `assertion_status` is `supported`;
3. at least one qualifying evidence record exists;
4. all referenced sources resolve;
5. the publication guidance permits the proposed use;
6. required attribution and caveats are preserved; and
7. no unresolved supersession applies.

Provisional, uncertain or disputed material may appear only through an explicit editorial component that exposes its status and has recorded human approval. It must not enter key facts, FAQs, metadata or structured data in a form that loses its qualification.

Rejected claims may be discussed only when explaining a misconception or the history of a story. Their rejected status must remain unmistakable.

## 7. Accuracy, precision and responsible wording

### 7.1 Match language to evidence

| Evidence position | Suitable wording |
|---|---|
| Direct and verified | "The register records..."; "The business advertised from..." |
| Strong inference | "The available evidence indicates..."; "The route appears to follow..." |
| Probable | "It was probably..."; "The evidence strongly suggests..." |
| Source report not independently verified | "The newspaper reported..."; "According to..." |
| Uncertain | "It has been suggested..., but no confirming record has yet been found." |
| Disputed | "The sources disagree..."; "One account states..., while..." |
| Earliest found evidence | "The earliest evidence identified in this research dates from..." |
| Approximate location | "Approximately on the site..."; "In the same general area..." |
| No result in a defined search | "No evidence was found in [named sources and range]." |

Words such as "proves", "certainly", "undoubtedly" and "definitively" should be reserved for the rare cases in which the evidence genuinely excludes responsible alternatives.

### 7.2 Do not turn a snapshot into a duration

A source dated 25 April 1952 establishes a position on or by that date. It does not by itself establish that the position began in 1952, continued throughout 1952 or persisted into 1953.

Use:

> Birkheads advertised from Church Street by April 1952.

Do not use, without further evidence:

> Birkheads opened in Church Street in 1952.

### 7.3 Dates and time

Historical dates must retain their actual precision.

- Use `17 July 1997` for an exact date.
- Use `July 1997` when only the month is known.
- Use `1997` when only the year is known.
- Use `c. 1920` for an honestly approximate year.
- Use `1867-68` for an evidenced year range.
- Use "before", "after", "by", "no later than" or "not earlier than" when those are the supported bounds.
- Do not convert an approximate year into 1 January of that year for technical convenience.

If the date of a source differs from the date represented, record both. A modern plan depicting an estate "about 1820" is a modern reconstruction of a historical period, not a map made in 1820.

### 7.4 Place, address and boundary precision

Addresses and boundaries change. The editor must distinguish:

- postal address from physical site;
- historic street name from current street name;
- plot number from house number;
- estate extent from surviving property;
- parish, ward, district and borough boundaries at the relevant date; and
- Walton-on-Thames from similarly named streets or places in neighbouring towns.

An address in an advertisement establishes the address used by the advertiser. It does not automatically establish legal title to the premises.

### 7.5 Names, aliases and spelling

Use the preferred, evidenced historical or current form appropriate to the context. Record variants and common errors separately.

- Use "Walton-on-Thames" in modern narrative unless quoting or citing a source that uses another form.
- Preserve the source's spelling in a quotation.
- Use `[sic]` sparingly. Prefer an explanatory note when the issue could distract or unfairly imply ignorance.
- Do not silently modernise a historically meaningful company, road, building or personal name.
- Do not infer that two differently named organisations are the same entity without evidence.

For the Sidney Road pilot, `Sidney Road` is the preferred name. `Sydney Road` is recorded as a common spelling error, not treated as an authorised historical name.

### 7.6 Numbers, prices and measurements

- Check every number against the source image or authoritative dataset.
- Distinguish an asking price, advertised price, valuation and completed sale price.
- Preserve historical currency in the first instance.
- If a modern-value comparison is added, name the method, comparator, conversion date and limitation.
- State whether a measurement is surveyed, mapped, calculated, estimated or quoted.
- Avoid false precision introduced by unit conversion.

## 8. Citation and reference house style

### 8.1 General rule

Walton-on-Thames.org uses a Harvard author-date system based on APA 7, adapted for archives, maps, photographs, newspapers, oral histories and other local-history sources.

Substantive claims must be cited close enough to make the relationship clear. A reference list at the bottom of a page is not sufficient if readers cannot tell which source supports which claim.

### 8.2 In-text citations

Use:

- parenthetical form: `(Stonebanks, 1982, p. 16)`;
- narrative form: `Stonebanks (1982, p. 16) states...`;
- organisation as author: `(Historic England, 2024)`;
- multiple sources: `(Hughes, 2003, pp. 44-45; Stonebanks, 1982, p. 16)`; and
- no named author: a short title and year, for example `("Way Back in Walton", 1997)`.

Page, column, figure, folio, map sheet or timestamp locators must be supplied when they materially help the reader verify the claim.

### 8.3 Citation placement

- Place a citation immediately after the claim or sentence it supports.
- A citation at the end of a paragraph may support the whole paragraph only when that relationship is genuinely unambiguous.
- Cite each materially distinct claim when a paragraph draws on several sources.
- Do not attach a citation to a sentence if the cited source supports only part of it.
- Captions require their own source and rights information. Do not assume the article bibliography covers image reuse.

### 8.4 Reference-list rules

Each entry must contain enough information to identify and retrieve the source. Where available, include author or issuing body, date, title, publication or repository, issue or edition, page, archival reference, stable URL and access date.

The `References` section should contain sources actually cited. Uncited but useful material may appear separately as `Further research` or remain in the WHKS source register.

### 8.5 Quotations and transcription

- Check every quotation visually against the original source.
- Preserve the original meaning, spelling and punctuation except for clearly marked editorial interventions.
- Use square brackets for editorial additions.
- Use ellipses only where omitted text does not alter the meaning.
- Mark unreadable text as `[illegible]` and uncertain readings as `[word?]`.
- Do not place reconstructed or paraphrased wording inside quotation marks.
- Keep quotations proportionate. Prefer concise quotation and responsible paraphrase over unnecessary reproduction.
- Record translation authorship and whether the translation was checked.

OCR is a finding aid. It must not be quoted or treated as checked transcription until compared with the rendered source.

## 9. Writing and narrative standards

### 9.1 Voice and language

Published work must use clear British English. It should be authoritative, calm, humane and accessible to a reader with no specialist knowledge.

The writing should:

- answer the reader's central question early;
- explain necessary local and historical context;
- use active, concrete sentences where appropriate;
- distinguish people, businesses, buildings and sites precisely;
- define specialist terms on first use;
- use chronology when it improves understanding;
- present uncertainty without burying it in footnotes;
- avoid invented scenes, dialogue, thoughts or motives; and
- remain engaging through evidence, character, place and consequence rather than fictionalisation.

### 9.2 Fact, inference, interpretation and recollection

Readers must be able to tell whether a passage describes:

- what a source directly records;
- an inference drawn from comparison;
- an editorial interpretation;
- a named source's opinion;
- a personal recollection;
- a local tradition; or
- an unresolved question.

Transitions such as "the record shows", "the map indicates", "this suggests", "according to", "the witness recalled" and "local tradition holds" should be used deliberately.

### 9.3 Headlines, summaries and standfirsts

Headlines and summaries must not be more certain or dramatic than the article. A question may be used as a headline when the evidence does not support a definitive answer.

Do not use clickbait, unsupported superlatives or an exact claim that the body text later retreats from.

### 9.4 Structure

A substantial historical article should normally include:

1. a concise answer or overview;
2. the subject's local significance;
3. the evidence and chronology;
4. important context and connections;
5. areas of uncertainty or dispute;
6. surviving evidence or present-day relevance, where appropriate;
7. references;
8. image credits and rights information; and
9. a published or last-reviewed date.

The exact structure should follow the needs of the subject rather than a rigid template.

### 9.5 Biographies

Biographical writing must:

- distinguish established identity from possible identity;
- avoid assuming motives without evidence;
- treat family members as people in their own right, not merely supporting characters;
- avoid present-day diagnosis of historical people without responsible evidence;
- distinguish contemporary reputation from later assessment; and
- identify the person's specific connection to Walton-on-Thames, Hersham or Whiteley Village.

### 9.6 Contested and uncomfortable history

The site must not romanticise the past or erase evidence of conflict, exclusion, exploitation, prejudice, poverty, war, death or institutional failure. Such subjects must be handled with proportion, context and humane language.

Historically offensive terminology should be reproduced only when its exact wording is necessary to understand the evidence. The surrounding text must not adopt or normalise it.

### 9.7 First-person and opinion content

Personal perspective may be valuable, particularly when explaining discovery, local experience or editorial judgement. It must be clearly identified and must not substitute for evidence.

Opinion, campaigning or recommendation content must be labelled and kept distinct from the site's historical conclusions.

## 10. Images, documents, maps and reconstructions

### 10.1 Every published visual requires a record

The editorial record must include:

- a descriptive title or identifier;
- what the visual shows;
- creator or source, where known;
- date or estimated period;
- original source and locator;
- rights holder, licence or permission basis;
- required credit line;
- crop or transformation status;
- any material uncertainty;
- alt text;
- public caption; and
- associated WHKS source, entity or claim identifiers where applicable.

### 10.2 Captions

A caption should state:

1. what is visible;
2. where it is;
3. the date or estimated period;
4. the viewpoint where useful;
5. the creator or source where known;
6. whether the image is original, restored, colourised, composite or reconstructed; and
7. any uncertainty needed to prevent misinterpretation.

Do not use a caption to smuggle in an unsupported claim.

### 10.3 Alt text and accessible alternatives

Alt text must serve the function of the image in its particular context. It should describe the historically relevant content without repeating the caption word for word.

- Decorative images should have empty alt text.
- Complex maps, charts, newspaper pages and documents should have a concise alt description plus an adjacent transcript, table or extended description when the detail matters.
- Text that is essential to the argument must not be available only inside an image.
- Audio and video require appropriate transcripts or captions.

The editorial content should support a target of WCAG 2.2 Level AA.

### 10.4 Restoration and enhancement

Faithful restoration may correct dust, scratches, fading, skew or tonal imbalance. It must not invent architectural detail, facial features, registration numbers, signs, people or scenery absent from the source.

Material transformations must be labelled using one of the following descriptions:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Original | An unaltered original or faithful scan, apart from routine format conversion |
| Cropped | Part of the original frame has been removed |
| Faithfully restored | Damage or reproduction defects were reduced without intentionally adding historical content |
| Colourised | Colour was added to a monochrome source and is interpretive |
| Composite | Elements from more than one source were combined |
| Reconstruction | Missing or unseen historical appearance was recreated from evidence and interpretation |
| AI-generated reconstruction | A generative system created some or all of the visual reconstruction |

The original must be retained. A reconstruction must never replace the original in the evidence record.

### 10.5 Maps and overlays

A published overlay must disclose the maps used, their dates, alignment method and likely error. It should include a legend, scale where meaningful, north indication where useful and an accessible text explanation of the conclusion.

Approximate georeferencing must not be displayed in a way that implies survey-grade precision.

## 11. Copyright, licensing and permissions

### 11.1 Rights are separate from historical value

A source may be highly valuable evidence but unavailable for public reproduction. The WHKS may record and describe it while the website publishes only permitted quotation, a reference or a separately licensed image.

### 11.2 Publication requires a documented basis

Before reproducing an image, document or substantial extract, record one of the following:

- permission from the relevant rights holder;
- a licence covering the proposed use;
- confirmed expiry of copyright;
- an applicable open licence or public-sector reuse licence;
- ownership by Walton-on-Thames.org or the contributor with authority to license it; or
- a documented legal exception reviewed as appropriate for the specific use.

"Found online", "old", "no copyright mark" and "held by an archive" are not rights statuses.

Archive access does not automatically include publication permission. Contractual archive or database terms may apply even where the underlying work is out of copyright.

### 11.3 Orphan and uncertain rights

Copyright does not disappear because the creator or owner cannot be found. Where ownership is uncertain:

- conduct and record a proportionate diligent search;
- consider an alternative image;
- consider whether an orphan-works licence or another lawful route is available; and
- do not label the work "public domain" without an adequate basis.

### 11.4 Credits and moral rights

Follow the credit wording required by the creator, archive, licence or donor. Preserve authorship metadata where practicable. Alterations must not be presented in a way that misrepresents or unfairly harms the creator's work.

### 11.5 Legal review

These Standards provide editorial controls, not legal advice. When the proposed use depends on a contested copyright exception, unclear ownership, an exclusivity term or a significant commercial risk, publication must pause for appropriate legal or specialist review.

## 12. Privacy, dignity, fairness and legal risk

### 12.1 Public availability is not automatic justification

Information should be published because it contributes meaningfully to the historical or public record, not merely because it can be found.

For living people, the editor must consider:

- accuracy and source reliability;
- relevance to the public or historical purpose;
- reasonable expectations of privacy;
- data minimisation;
- potential harm;
- whether the person is a child or vulnerable adult;
- whether sensitive personal data is involved;
- whether the information is already genuinely public and in context;
- fairness and opportunity to respond; and
- the lawful basis and safeguards for processing where required.

### 12.2 High-risk material

The following requires explicit senior editorial review and, where appropriate, specialist legal advice:

- allegations of crime, dishonesty, abuse, discrimination or serious professional failure;
- statements likely to cause serious reputational harm;
- active legal proceedings or material subject to reporting restrictions;
- identification of victims of sexual offences;
- information about children;
- private medical, genetic, financial or family information;
- precise private addresses or contact information without clear public relevance;
- suicide reporting;
- covertly obtained or leaked private material;
- confidential sources; and
- any request to publish despite a credible warning of harm or illegality.

### 12.3 Fair opportunity to respond

Where publication contains significant criticism or an allegation affecting a living person or operating organisation, they should normally be offered a fair opportunity to respond to the specific point and given a reasonable time appropriate to the circumstances.

Their response does not create a right to suppress accurate evidence. Relevant response and supporting material must be considered fairly.

### 12.4 Grief, death, war and disaster

Accounts of death, conflict, disaster and personal hardship must be accurate, necessary and humane. Avoid sensational detail, gratuitous imagery and language that treats suffering as entertainment.

Approaches to bereaved people must be made with sympathy and discretion. A refusal to engage must be respected.

### 12.5 Data protection

Research involving identifiable living people must observe the principles of lawfulness, fairness and transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security and accountability.

Historical-research or archiving provisions must not be assumed to create a blanket exemption. Where relied upon, the applicable conditions and safeguards must be recorded.

### 12.6 Defamation and opinion

Potentially defamatory claims must never be published simply because a source has already printed them. The editor must assess what the site itself would communicate, the quality of the evidence, context, public interest, fairness and the distinction between fact and opinion.

Opinion must be clearly presented as opinion and must identify its factual basis. A label such as "opinion" cannot protect an unsupported factual allegation embedded within it.

## 13. Community contributions and confidential material

### 13.1 Intake record

For a contributed photograph, document, memory or correction, record:

- contributor name and contact route;
- date received;
- description of the material;
- provenance and how the contributor obtained it;
- claimed creator or rights holder;
- permission, licence and credit terms;
- privacy or embargo restrictions;
- whether return of originals is required;
- related WHKS entities and claims; and
- verification work still needed.

### 13.2 Ownership and permission

Sharing material for consideration does not transfer copyright or ownership. The proposed public use must be agreed clearly.

### 13.3 Respectful verification

Contributors should be thanked and treated fairly. Verification is not a sign of distrust. It protects the contributor, the site and the historical record.

### 13.4 Confidential sources

Promises of confidentiality must not be made casually. If granted, the editor must record the reason, what may be published, who knows the identity and how the identifying information will be protected.

Anonymity does not lower the evidential threshold. Material allegations from a confidential source require strong corroboration and senior editorial approval.

## 14. Responsible use of AI and automated tools

### 14.1 Permitted assistance

AI and automated tools may assist with:

- source discovery and search-term generation;
- OCR and transcription drafts;
- entity and alias suggestions;
- comparison, deduplication and data organisation;
- extraction of candidate dates, names and relationships;
- draft outlines and prose;
- citation formatting;
- metadata, alt-text and accessibility suggestions;
- translation drafts;
- image restoration or clearly labelled reconstruction; and
- quality-assurance checks.

### 14.2 Prohibited reliance

An AI system must not:

- serve as the source of a historical claim;
- invent or complete a quotation, citation, archive reference or missing text;
- turn OCR into checked evidence without visual comparison;
- approve a WHKS record or public page;
- suppress contrary evidence to make a narrative cleaner;
- infer exact locations, dates or identities from weak resemblance;
- publish generated historical imagery as an original record;
- expose uncertain claims as verified structured data;
- make final legal, privacy, rights or safeguarding decisions; or
- mass-publish pages without individual evidential and editorial review.

### 14.3 Mandatory verification

Every AI-assisted substantive claim must be traced to real evidence and checked against the original source. Generated citations must be treated as untrusted until resolved and inspected.

Material AI use should be recorded in project notes when it affects transcription, translation, evidence extraction, image transformation or the form of a published reconstruction. Routine spelling or grammar assistance does not normally require public disclosure.

### 14.4 AI-generated and reconstructed images

Any image materially generated or reconstructed by AI must be clearly labelled at the point of use. The caption must explain that it is a reconstruction and identify the evidence used. It must not be placed or described in a way that a reasonable reader could mistake for an original photograph or document.

## 15. Search, accessibility and machine-readable publishing

### 15.1 People first

Search optimisation exists to help readers discover strong work. Content must be created primarily for people, not to manipulate rankings.

The site should:

- answer genuine questions in clear language;
- demonstrate original research and local expertise;
- use accurate titles and descriptive headings;
- include the words readers reasonably use for the subject;
- provide meaningful internal and external links;
- keep content reviewed and corrected;
- avoid keyword stuffing, cloned pages and thin summaries; and
- avoid automation at a scale that removes editorial value.

### 15.2 Search summaries and metadata

Page titles, descriptions, social cards, excerpts and search snippets must preserve the conclusion's real certainty. A metadata field must not state as fact what the article calls probable or uncertain.

### 15.3 Structured data

Structured data may include only claims that are approved, supported and accurately representable in the selected vocabulary.

- Do not invent a relationship to fill a Schema.org field.
- Do not publish an approximate site as an exact address or coordinate.
- Do not expose a disputed naming origin as a settled fact.
- Do not create FAQ answers solely to obtain a search feature.
- Preserve uncertainty in visible prose even where the public schema cannot express the full WHKS model.

The absence of a suitable public schema type is not a reason to distort the historical relationship. The richer relationship remains in the WHKS.

### 15.4 Accessibility

Editorial content must support accessible headings, descriptive link text, useful alt text, captions, transcripts, table headings and plain-language explanations. Colour, position or visual appearance must not be the sole means of conveying a historical distinction.

## 16. Editorial workflow and publication gates

### Stage 1. Brief and scope

The research question, boundaries, risks and output are recorded.

### Stage 2. Source discovery and registration

Relevant sources are identified, registered and assessed. Search coverage and inaccessible material are noted.

### Stage 3. Evidence extraction

Relevant pages and visual regions are inspected. Candidate entities, atomic claims, evidence records, events, relationships and gaps are added to the WHKS.

### Stage 4. Evidence checking

A human checker compares each material observation with its source locator and reviews identity, date precision, claim scope, evidence strength and contradictions.

### Stage 5. Claim approval

The accountable editor decides assertion status, confidence, public evidence state, publication wording, caveats and attribution. Only then may the record be marked `approved`.

### Stage 6. Editorial drafting

The writer uses the approved claim trail. Provisional, disputed, traditional or folkloric material may be included only with the permitted status and wording.

### Stage 7. Fact-check and integrity review

The draft is checked claim by claim for names, dates, quotations, numbers, locations, source fit, missing conflicts and certainty language.

### Stage 8. Rights, privacy, sensitivity and accessibility review

Images, quotations, personal information, legal risk, captions, alt text and disclosures are checked.

### Stage 9. WKA and technical review

The page's URL, entities, timeline, internal links, metadata, structured data and generated modules are checked against approved WHKS records. The site build and relevant automated tests must pass.

### Stage 10. Human publication approval

The Founder and Editor, or a formally delegated human editor, gives final approval. No AI tool may perform this step.

### Stage 11. Continuing stewardship

The page receives publication and review dates. New evidence, challenges, broken links and time-sensitive hybrid content are monitored. Corrections begin in the WHKS claim trail before the durable page is updated, except where urgent removal is necessary to prevent harm.

## 17. Review roles and separation of duties

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Researcher | Finds, registers and assesses sources; identifies claims and gaps |
| Evidence checker | Compares source observations and quotations with the original item |
| Writer | Produces clear narrative from the permitted knowledge state |
| Fact-checker | Tests the draft against claims, evidence, conflicts and wording guidance |
| Rights and sensitivity reviewer | Checks permissions, privacy, dignity and higher-risk material |
| Technical publisher | Implements the approved page through the WKA without changing the editorial meaning |
| Approving editor | Accepts final human responsibility for publication |

One person may perform several roles in a small independent project. For a significant, disputed or high-risk conclusion, a second human review should be sought where reasonably possible.

An AI tool may assist any stage except final approval. It may not be recorded as the accountable reviewer.

Conflicts of interest must be declared. A contributor, business, sponsor or subject may provide evidence, but must not control the conclusion drawn from it.

## 18. Corrections, challenges and new evidence

### 18.1 Corrections are part of the historical method

Revision in response to better evidence is a strength. It must be prompt, proportionate and traceable.

### 18.2 Types of change

| Change | Meaning | Normal treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Minor edit | Spelling, grammar, formatting or accessibility change that does not alter meaning | Correct without a public notice; retain normal version history |
| Clarification | Wording is accurate but could reasonably mislead or lacks important context | Amend and add a note when material |
| Factual correction | A factual statement was materially wrong | Correct promptly, update WHKS and publish a visible correction note |
| Conclusion revision | New evidence changes the interpretation, certainty or central conclusion | Supersede affected WHKS records, revise the article and explain what changed |
| Retraction | The article or a substantial section cannot responsibly remain published | Remove or replace it, retain an internal record and publish an appropriate notice |
| Routine update | New information extends rather than corrects the account | Add the evidence, update review date and note the update when useful |

### 18.3 Severity and response

- **Critical:** credible risk of serious harm, illegality, false identification, major rights breach or dangerously misleading content. Remove or hold the affected material immediately while reviewing it.
- **Major:** the error changes the article's central conclusion, chronology, identity, location, ownership or reputation. Correct promptly and publish a visible notice.
- **Moderate:** the error or omission affects a supporting point or could mislead a careful reader. Correct and consider a clarification note.
- **Minor:** no material change of meaning. Correct in normal maintenance.

### 18.4 Correction workflow

1. Record the challenge and evidence received.
2. Acknowledge it where contact details are available.
3. Preserve the current published state and relevant records.
4. Assess the evidence without giving preference to status, popularity or pressure.
5. Update, dispute, reject or retain the WHKS claim with a reason and reviewer.
6. Identify every affected page, timeline, caption, FAQ, map, metadata field and structured-data record.
7. Publish the correction, clarification or response with suitable prominence.
8. Retain the earlier state in version control.

### 18.5 Right of response

A person or organisation affected by a material inaccuracy should be given a fair opportunity to provide evidence or a response. The final judgement remains evidence-led and independent.

## 19. Minimum metadata for every published historical page

The WKA may choose the technical field names, but every published historical page must preserve these concepts:

- canonical title and URL;
- content type;
- geographic scope;
- principal WHKS entity identifiers;
- source and claim identifiers used;
- author or writer;
- fact-checker or reviewing editor;
- approving editor;
- first-publication date;
- last substantive revision date;
- last fact-check date;
- public evidence-state summary where useful;
- rights review status;
- sensitivity or legal review status where required;
- correction or supersession status;
- related entities and pages; and
- next review date where the page includes changing information.

These fields must not imply that a page is complete, current or reviewed when the required work has not occurred.

## 20. Pre-publication checklist

The approving editor must record `pass`, `fail` or `not applicable` for each relevant item.

### Scope and identity

- [ ] The central question is answered at the level the evidence permits.
- [ ] The page concerns the correct Walton-on-Thames, Hersham, Whiteley Village or connected place.
- [ ] People and organisations have been disambiguated.
- [ ] Company, brand, occupier, building and site have not been conflated.
- [ ] Preferred names and meaningful aliases are correct.

### Claims and evidence

- [ ] Every significant claim has a WHKS claim and evidence trail.
- [ ] Each claim is atomic enough to review independently.
- [ ] Source locators are precise and resolvable.
- [ ] OCR-derived material has been checked visually.
- [ ] Evidence strength and source role are appropriate to the claim.
- [ ] Credible conflicting evidence is recorded and represented fairly.
- [ ] Corroboration has been sought for significant claims.
- [ ] Negative and exhaustive claims have a defined search scope.
- [ ] Rejected or unknown claims have not been promoted into narrative fact.

### Accuracy and wording

- [ ] Names, dates, addresses, numbers and quotations match the sources.
- [ ] Date and spatial precision have not been inflated.
- [ ] A snapshot has not been turned into an unsupported duration.
- [ ] "First", "last", "only", "oldest", "never" and similar wording is justified.
- [ ] Fact, inference, interpretation, recollection and tradition remain distinguishable.
- [ ] Headlines, summaries and metadata do not overstate the body text.
- [ ] The conclusion acknowledges material uncertainty and gaps.

### Citations and quotations

- [ ] Substantive claims have citations close enough to be unambiguous.
- [ ] Reference entries are complete enough to retrieve the source.
- [ ] Quotations were checked against the original and remain in context.
- [ ] Archive, page, column, figure, folio or timestamp locators are included where useful.
- [ ] Translations and transcriptions identify their responsible creator or checker.

### Images and maps

- [ ] Each visual has a source, date, caption, alt text and rights record.
- [ ] Material cropping, restoration, colourisation, compositing or reconstruction is disclosed.
- [ ] The original visual has been retained.
- [ ] A reconstruction cannot reasonably be mistaken for an original record.
- [ ] Map alignment and location wording match the real spatial precision.
- [ ] Complex visuals have an accessible text alternative.

### Rights, privacy and fairness

- [ ] Reproduction has a documented permission, licence, expiry or reviewed exception.
- [ ] Archive or database terms have been checked.
- [ ] Personal information is relevant, proportionate and handled securely.
- [ ] Children, grief, health, crime, allegations and vulnerable people received enhanced review.
- [ ] Significant criticism received a fair opportunity for response where appropriate.
- [ ] Conflicts of interest and commercial relationships are disclosed.
- [ ] Specialist review was obtained for material legal or rights risk.

### Accessibility, search and technical publication

- [ ] Headings are logical and descriptive.
- [ ] Link text is meaningful.
- [ ] The page provides a useful answer for a human reader.
- [ ] Search terms occur naturally rather than through stuffing.
- [ ] Structured data contains only approved, supported and accurately modelled claims.
- [ ] Internal links and related-entity relationships resolve correctly.
- [ ] The canonical URL and redirects are correct.
- [ ] The site build and relevant validation tests pass.

### Approval and stewardship

- [ ] Author, checker and approving editor are recorded.
- [ ] Publication, fact-check and review dates are accurate.
- [ ] Open research gaps remain recorded in the WHKS.
- [ ] The final human approver has confirmed publication.

Any failed mandatory item blocks publication until resolved or until an explicit, Charter-compliant editorial exception is recorded. No exception may conceal uncertainty, waive human approval or permit unlawful publication.

## Appendix A. Citation templates

These templates show the house pattern. Adapt them to the information actually available. Do not invent missing bibliographic detail.

### Book or local-history study

`Author, A. A. (Year). *Title*. Publisher.`

In text: `(Author, Year, p. 00)`

### Journal or society paper

`Author, A. A. (Year). Title of article. *Journal or Society Transactions, volume*(issue), page-page.`

### Newspaper report

`Author, A. A. (Year, Day Month). Title of article. *Newspaper*, p. 00, col. 0.`

If no named author:

`Title of article. (Year, Day Month). *Newspaper*, p. 00.`

### Newspaper advertisement

`Advertiser. (Year, Day Month). [Advertisement]. *Newspaper*, p. 00, region or column.`

### Archive item

`Creator. (Date). *Item title or description* [Document type]. Collection, reference, item or folio. Repository, place.`

### Map or plan

`Cartographer or issuing body. (Year). *Title* [Map, edition, sheet, scale]. Publisher or repository, reference.`

### Photograph

`Creator or Unknown photographer. (Date or c. date). *Description* [Photograph]. Collection, reference. Rights holder.`

### Census or register

`Responsible body. (Year). *Census or register title*, place, schedule, page or entry [Dataset or image]. Repository or database.`

### Planning or heritage record

`Authority. (Year). *Application, decision or list-entry title*, reference. URL (accessed Day Month Year).`

### Web page

`Organisation or Author. (Year or n.d.). *Page title*. URL (accessed Day Month Year).`

Use an archived URL as well when the historical state of a changing page matters.

### Oral history

`Interviewee. (Year, Day Month). Interview by Interviewer [Audio recording or transcript], timestamp or section. Collection. Permission status.`

### Contributed unpublished material

`Contributor. (Date). *Description* [Unpublished photograph, document or written recollection]. Private collection. Used with permission.`

## Appendix B. Source-specific worked examples from the foundation corpus

These examples demonstrate what a source can and cannot establish. They are methodological examples, not completed WHKS conclusions about the businesses concerned.

### Birkheads advertisement, 25 April 1952

The Birkheads advertisement on page 2 of *The Herald and News* identifies `Church St., Walton` and promotes radio and television, prams, cycles and sports goods.

It can support:

- that Birkheads publicly advertised a Church Street, Walton presence on 25 April 1952; and
- that it advertised the listed product categories on that date.

It cannot by itself support:

- the exact numbered address;
- the opening or closing date;
- ownership of the premises;
- uninterrupted trading before or after the date; or
- the truth of the promotional description "all leading makes".

### Flatley stockist advertisement, 29 August 1960

The national Flatley advertisement in the *Daily Mirror* includes a Walton stockist entry for `W. Birkhead & Son Ltd., 24 Church Street`.

It can support that the advertiser listed that company and address as a Flatley stockist on the publication date. It does not by itself establish the whole period of dealership, site ownership or the stock physically present in the shop.

### Shephards Solicitors property advertisement, 20 March 1986

The *Walton & Weybridge Informer* advertisement identifies the property department at Admiral Rodney House, 17 Church Street, Walton-on-Thames, and gives asking prices for listed properties.

It can support the public use of that business address and the advertised asking prices on that date. It does not establish completed sale prices, legal ownership of Admiral Rodney House or the accuracy of every property description.

### Safeway recruitment advertisement, 20 March 1986

The Safeway advertisement in the *Walton & Weybridge Informer* recruits staff for its Walton-on-Thames store.

It can support that Safeway was publicly recruiting for a Walton-on-Thames store on that date. Without another source, it should not be used to establish the exact address, a store-opening date, whether every vacancy was filled or the precise operational status of the premises.

### "Way Back in Walton", 17 July 1997

The Leader feature combines a reproduced historical photograph, a caption dating the view to about 1920 and a later narrative about Bridge Street businesses.

The photograph, its caption and the 1997 prose are separate evidence objects. The photograph can be analysed for visible features. The caption records a later approximate date and identification. The prose is a secondary local-history account that should be traced to its named or underlying sources where a claim is significant.

### *The Evening Star*, 22 August 1815

A contemporary newspaper report is primary evidence of what the paper reported in 1815. Its age and proximity do not automatically verify every detail. A significant event should still be checked against independent newspapers, official papers, correspondence, diaries or other contemporary records where reasonably available.

## Appendix C. Non-negotiable instructions for automated agents

Any automated or AI-assisted process working on Walton-on-Thames.org history must follow these rules:

1. Do not invent a fact, quotation, citation, person, relationship, date, address or archive reference.
2. Do not use an AI answer, search snippet or unchecked OCR as evidence.
3. Register the real source and preserve its provenance before approving a claim.
4. Express one independently testable proposition per WHKS claim.
5. Link every approved substantive claim to precise evidence.
6. Preserve fact, inference, interpretation and recollection as different claim types.
7. Preserve supported, provisional, disputed, rejected and unknown assertion states.
8. Do not infer that `checked` means `approved`.
9. Do not publish a draft or checked record as unqualified fact.
10. Do not turn a source date into an unsupported opening, closing or duration claim.
11. Do not turn an approximate location into an exact address or coordinate.
12. Do not merge a company, brand, occupier, building, site or successor without evidence.
13. Do not treat repeated copies of one account as independent corroboration.
14. Record conflicting evidence and unresolved gaps.
15. Preserve required attribution and caveats in prose, metadata and structured data.
16. Do not publish uncertain or disputed claims automatically.
17. Do not create structured data that is more certain than the visible article.
18. Check every quotation and material transcription against the rendered original.
19. Record rights status separately from evidential value.
20. Label every material reconstruction, colourisation, composite or AI-generated historical image.
21. Do not expose unnecessary personal data or make final legal and safeguarding decisions.
22. Do not grant final publication approval. Record the accountable human editor.
23. If new evidence changes a conclusion, update the WHKS record first and preserve the superseded state.
24. If the Charter and a lower-level instruction conflict, stop and apply the Charter.
